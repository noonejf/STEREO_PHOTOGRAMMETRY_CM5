\chapter{The orbital illumination the session stands in for}
\label{chap:sun}

The studio arrangement of Chapter \ref{chap:simulation} is a stand-in. What
it stands in for is a tether in orbit lit by the Sun, and the session is only
worth running if the condition it reproduces is one the flight case actually
presents. In the studio the light hangs wherever it is hung. In orbit it
does not: the Sun arrives from where it arrives, and the geometry is given,
not chosen.

This chapter bounds that. It is the second half of the same simulation
campaign, run on 24 November 2026, and it answers a question asked in review
on 21 November: the project had swept the Sun around \emph{one} circle, and a
circle is not a sphere.

\textbf{Status.} Simulated, like Chapter \ref{chap:simulation}. A Blender Sun
object, no room, the IMX477 with the 8~mm, a wavy 24.5~m cord, 24 samples at
full sensor resolution. The tether and the stereo pair do not move; what
turns is the beam. \textbf{112 directions}: the complete 16 azimuths by 7
inclinations grid, poles included. The poles were a single azimuth each
until 25 November 2026; the other 15 of each were rendered then, and closing
that gap is what Section \ref{sec:sun-poles} now reports as measured rather
than assumed.

\section{The convention, which has to be stated before any number}

The beam travels along $(\cos t \sin o,\ -\sin t,\ -\cos t \cos o)$, with $o$
the azimuth and $t$ the inclination. At $o = 0$ and $t = 0$ the Sun comes
from directly above, perpendicular to the tether. At $o = 90$\textdegree\ it
comes from the $-x$ side; at $o = 180$\textdegree, from below. The
inclination tips the beam \textbf{along the tether itself}: positive, the Sun
is on the side of the far end and the beam travels towards the cameras;
negative, the Sun is behind the cameras.

\section{What the Sun in these renders is, and what it is not}
\label{sec:sun-what}

Four of its properties are the orbital ones and are not adjustable. The
source is a Blender Sun, so the rays are \textbf{parallel} and there is no
$1/r^2$ falloff, which is the one physical difference between this chapter
and the studio of Chapter \ref{chap:simulation}. Its colour is a
\textbf{5772~K} blackbody, the Sun's effective temperature. Its angular
diameter is \textbf{0.526\textdegree}, the real figure seen from Earth
orbit, and it is what makes the shadow edges as hard as they are in space.
The world emits \textbf{nothing}: Blender ships a grey world that acts as
uniform ambient light arriving from every direction, which is the one thing
space does not have, and leaving it on would quietly fill the shadowed side
of the cord.

The fifth property, the \textbf{scale}, is deliberately not the orbital one
and this has to be said plainly. These renders were \textbf{not} made at
AM0. The Sun is set to 36.89~W/m\textsuperscript{2}, which is
\textbf{3600~lx} at the cord, and 3600~lx is requirement R5, the same
illuminance the studio row of Chapter \ref{chap:simulation} is solved to
deliver. AM0 is 1361~W/m\textsuperscript{2}, about 132\,800~lx, a factor of
37 higher. Every capture's metadata records both figures, as
\texttt{irradiance\_w\_m2} and \texttt{irradiance\_am0\_w\_m2}.

\textbf{The choice does not change a single pixel, and that is exact rather
than approximate.} The exposure is solved from the same target illuminance
that sets the Sun. The rendered radiance is proportional to $E/K$, with $K$
the luminous efficacy, and the exposure multiplier is $\pi K / E$ by
construction (\texttt{exposure\_for} in
\texttt{SIMULATION/build\_tv2\_studio.py}), so the product is $\pi$ and the
illuminance cancels out of the image. Rendering the same geometry at AM0
with its own exposure returns the same frame. What is being reported here
is therefore the contrast of the cord \textbf{at a common 3600~lx working
point}, which is precisely what makes it comparable with the studio.

Two things it consequently does not answer, and neither is a defect of the
sweep so much as a boundary of it. It says nothing about \textbf{sensor
saturation or absolute signal-to-noise at AM0}, because the absolute signal
level was normalised away; answering that needs the sensor model, not the
renderer. And the \textbf{starfield is an artifact of the chosen scale}: its
emission is fixed rather than scaled with the Sun, and its strength was
tuned to put a single star just above the floor at this exposure, so at an
AM0 exposure the stars would simply disappear. They contribute 1\% of a
frame mean of 0.26 levels and nothing measurable on the cord, so no number
in this chapter depends on them.

Twelve directions keep the whole tether visible, and all twelve sit at
azimuth 22.5 or 45\textdegree\ with any inclination between $-60$ and
$+60$\textdegree. Twelve of 112 is 11\%, but that percentage is a property of
how the sphere was sampled and not of the sphere: the grid gives a pole the
same sixteen rows as the equator while it covers a far smaller solid angle,
so adding the poles lowers the fraction without any direction having
changed. What does not depend on the sampling is that none of the twelve is
at a pole, and that every one of them is at a small azimuth.

\textbf{That the contrast maximum and the visibility maximum do not coincide
is not a contradiction.} At azimuth 67.5\textdegree\ the tether is brighter
on average, 58.2 levels, but its far end has already begun to fade: 3.7\% of
it is blind against 0.0\% at 22.5\textdegree. For a measurement of arc
length, losing no stretch matters more than shining.

\textbf{What this says about the sweep that preceded it.} The ring already
answered the question that matters. The sphere does not contradict it, it
confirms it, and the confirmation is now measured rather than assumed. That
is the whole value of the exercise, and it is worth recording that the
reviewer who asked for it was right to ask.

\section{The two poles are not symmetric, and that is the useful result}

\label{sec:sun-poles}

At $\pm 89$\textdegree\ the beam runs nearly parallel to the tether, and the
azimuth stops meaning anything. That sentence used to be an assumption
resting on one render per pole; both poles were rendered at all sixteen
azimuths on 25 November 2026 and it now rests on the measurement.
\textbf{At $-89$\textdegree\ the contrast spans 24.8 to 27.0 levels over the
sixteen, a spread of 8\% about its mean, against 65 to 80\% at every other
inclination.} At $+89$\textdegree\ the spread is 47\%, but of figures between
0.5 and 0.7 levels: the tether is lost at every azimuth and the spread is
noise on nothing. So in the grazing case the inclination decides and the
azimuth does not, which is what the single sample had claimed.

The two directions are physically opposite and they give opposite results.

With the Sun behind the cameras the tether is still measurable: 26 levels of
contrast and 10\% blind, worse than the best case but usable, and that holds
at every azimuth. With the Sun at the far end, into the camera,
\textbf{between 79 and 84\% of the tether is lost} and what remains carries
half a level, again at every azimuth.

\textbf{This is the case that has to be recognised in flight, because it is
not chosen.} If the orbital geometry puts the Sun behind the tether as seen
from the cameras, the measurement is not poor, it is impossible, and knowing
that in advance is worth more than attempting it. A detector for the
condition is cheap: at $+89$\textdegree\ the usable arc collapses, so the
fraction of the traced path that carries any contrast at all is itself the
alarm.

\section{How to read these numbers}

Before the rules: the 112 directions can be browsed rather than read. The
application's \textbf{Simulation} page, tab \emph{Sol: la esfera}, draws each
direction on the scene it was rendered in, next to the cord and the stereo
pair, and shows the frame and the four figures for whichever one is picked.
It reads the same CSV this chapter's tables come from and recomputes
nothing, so the two cannot disagree. It needs no camera and no Blender.


\textbf{Contrast} is in 8-bit levels above the local background, averaged
from 5~m onward and not from the start of the tether. The cord begins at the
plane of the lens and its first two metres carry 1400 levels against 60 at
20~m, so including them would make the average a measure of distance rather
than of light.

\textbf{Blind tether} is the fraction of the \emph{whole} tether below 5
levels, which is where the mask has nothing left to segment. That one is
counted from the start, because the question it answers is what fraction of
the tether cannot be segmented, and the near stretch is part of the tether.

Both definitions and the threshold live in one place,
\texttt{tools/medir\_barrido.py}, and the sphere imports that function rather
than copying it. The same function produced the studio figures of Chapter
\ref{chap:simulation}, so the two chapters are on one scale.
