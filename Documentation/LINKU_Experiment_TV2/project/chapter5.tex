\chapter{The session simulated before it is run}
\label{chap:simulation}

Until 23 November 2026 this document specified the illumination as a set of
properties a fixture had to have, because the fixtures were unknown. On that
date the studio answered the inventory question: TV2 holds \textbf{four
Aputure STORM 1200x and two Aputure LS 1200d Pro}, and nothing else of that
class. Every simulation the project had run until then lit the tether with
twelve $1 \times 1$~m soft panels, which was an assumption of ours and not a
description of the room.

The room was therefore rebuilt in Blender with the fixtures that exist, and
the whole family of arrangements was rendered on 24 November 2026. This
chapter records what came out. It is written here, before the procedure, for
one reason: three of its results change an instruction in Chapter
\ref{chap:procedure}, and one of them reverses an instruction the project had
been following.

\textbf{Status of everything in this chapter.} It is \emph{simulated}, not
measured in the room. Cycles renders at 24 samples and full sensor
resolution, $4056 \times 3040$, with the photometry traced from the
manufacturer's candela figures. The session's own photometer readings
supersede every number here, and Section \ref{sec:sim-open} lists what has to
be measured before any of it is trusted. What the simulation is good for is
the opposite of a measurement: it is the only way to compare arrangements
that will never both be built.

Two properties of this inventory decide the montage before any render is
made.

\textbf{These are projectors, not panels.} A $1 \times 1$~m panel at 2~m above
the tether bathes its own stretch; a 45\textdegree\ cone at 2~m lights a
circle 1.66~m across and leaves the rest dark. The consequence is not that
less light arrives, it is that the row has to be \emph{higher} and the tether
\emph{lower} so that the cone footprints overlap. That is why the geometry
below puts the fixtures at 6.0~m and the tether at 1.5~m, and why the floor
enters the frame, which in turn is why Section \ref{sec:sim-cloth} exists.

\textbf{Mixing the two models along one row is allowed here and nowhere
else.} Parameter L1 forbids mixing fixture types along a row, and the
recommended arrangement mixes four STORM with one LS. The prohibition is
about the \emph{direction and colour} of the light, not about the badge: all
five carry the same 45\textdegree\ reflector, all five point the same way,
and the STORM units are set to 5600~K to match the LS, which is fixed there.
What differs is only on-axis intensity, 178\,592 against 128\,365~cd, and
that shows up as one dimmer stretch rather than as a second shadow. Where
that stretch falls is not left to chance: the row is ordered by intensity
from the outside in, so the weakest unit lands in the middle, where it gets
the most help from both of its neighbours. With four equal STORM units and
one LS that puts the LS at $y = 11.0$~m.

\section{The beam points straight down}
\label{sec:sim-beam}

This is the result that reverses an instruction. Every simulation the project
had run tilted the beam 40\textdegree, and the argument written into the
scene builder was that a beam from above lights the \emph{top} face of the
cylinder while the cameras look at its \emph{side}, so the beam should be
tilted to put light where the cameras are looking.

That argument holds in the side view and says nothing about the axis view,
which is the primary one. There the camera looks \emph{along} the tether,
which stays within three degrees of the optical axis, so of each stretch it
does not see the side of the cylinder: it sees the \textbf{limb}, whose
normals point along $x$ and along $z$. A vertical beam lights the $+z$ normal,
which is on the limb. A tilted beam spends part of its flux on a normal along
$y$, which is not visible from anywhere.

\textbf{The direction of the tilt barely matters; tilting is what costs.}
Towards the cameras and away from them differ by 3.5 levels, while either one
differs from vertical by more than twenty.

\textbf{It is not a shortage of light.} Measured with the point source model,
tilting 40\textdegree\ does not lower the worst point, it raises it slightly,
from 4419 to 4597~lx, because an oblique cone stretches its footprint along
the tether. So the contrast that tilting costs is not light that failed to
arrive, it is light placed on a face the camera cannot see.

\textbf{It does not lose in the side view either.} There the cord ties, 93.6
vertical against 94.6 tilted, and what changes is the background: mean 9.8
levels with 20\% of the frame lit, against 16.5 with 56\%. Tilting the beam
towards the camera throws light onto the floor and the wall that are in shot.

\textbf{And it keeps the fixture out of frame, which is parameter L7.}
Counted on the rendered frames, pixels above level 230 number zero with the
vertical beam, zero with the beam tilted away from the cameras, and
\textbf{3064 with the beam tilted towards them}, which is one fixture inside
the field of view. The twelve-panel arrangement puts 157\,838 such pixels in
the frame, 1.28\% of it, which is a whole panel in shot. L7 was written as a
rigging instruction; here it turns out to follow from the aiming decision.

\textbf{What this saves on the day.} With a vertical beam each fixture hangs
directly above the stretch it lights, instead of 3.78~m upstream of it. It is
easier to rig, easier to check with a plumb line, and easier to explain to
the studio technician.

The mean background triples. The cord contrast \emph{rises} with the bare
floor, because a pale floor bounces light back onto the cord, and that is not
an argument for the wood: what the mask has to solve is not the local
contrast of the cord but how much bright background has to be discarded
before reaching it. A 50-level floor filling the lower third of a
$4056 \times 3040$ frame is the dominant feature of the image, and the
segmenter was neither trained nor tuned for that.

Two cautions on these two rows. The albedos are assumed, 0.04 and 0.30. And
varnished wood is not Lambertian: it sends a specular reflection towards the
camera that this model does not reproduce, so 0.30 diffuse is the optimistic
version of the problem.

Three readings of that table are worth stating.

\textbf{The five real fixtures beat the twelve assumed panels}, 112.9 against
68.2, with two kilowatts less. This is not five projectors out-lighting
twelve panels: it is that a projector concentrates its flux into a cone while
a panel spreads it over a hemisphere, and in the arithmetic of the row that
outweighs the unit count. What the twelve panels buy is uniformity, and
uniformity is not what limits the measurement here.

\textbf{The IMX296 with its 6~mm loses by half}, 44.4 against 91.1 in the
same room. This agrees with the orbital sweep of Chapter \ref{chap:sun},
where it gave 24.3 against 50.0 averaged over sixteen azimuths. The cause is
not noise: the cord subtends 0.58~px at 3~m against 1.72~px for the IMX477
with its 8~mm, which is the optical constraint already recorded for that
sensor.

\textbf{The taut tether scores lower than the wavy one}, 76.8 against 77.3,
and the two contrast definitions coincide for it. A straight cord lies in one
plane and receives the same grazing illumination along its whole length,
while the wave carries parts of the cord into the bright centre of a
footprint. This matters for the session only as a warning not to read the
tether's contrast as a property of the lighting alone.

\section{The photometric chain closes}
\label{sec:sim-photometry}

Every number above rests on a chain that runs from the manufacturer's candela
figure, through watts in Blender, to a pixel level. The chain was checked at
both ends, because a silent factor anywhere in it would invalidate the whole
chapter.

\textbf{The candela to watt conversion of a spot, measured and not assumed.}
A Blender spot has intensity $P/(4\pi)$~W/sr whether or not it has a cone:
narrowing the cone masks the beam, it does not concentrate it. A white
Lambertian plane at 5~m under a spot of $4\pi$~W returned 0.012715 against
the 0.012732 that the isotropic reading predicts, a ratio of 0.9986, and a
point light of the same power gave an identical pixel. The factor between the
plausible conventions is 4, so getting it wrong would have rendered
everything here four times too dark.

\textbf{The lux, recovered from the floor.} The molton is a Lambertian plane
of known albedo, so a linear OpenEXR render can be inverted with
$E = \pi L / \rho$ and converted from radiometric to photometric with a
luminous efficacy of 97.58~lm/W. Over twenty-four floor points between 7.1
and 20.4~m:

The render delivers about 30\% more than the model. That is the direction and
the order of magnitude the model itself predicts, since it ignores wall
bounce and truncates the tail of the beam where the data sheet has one. The
spread reflects the other known difference: the model extends the beam with a
raised cosine, $n = 8.70$ for a 45\textdegree\ beam, while the scene uses a
cone of 75\textdegree\ field angle with a blend of 0.8. The two agree at the
half-peak point by construction and not in the tail, and the tail is exactly
what fills the gaps between fixtures.

\textbf{So Table \ref{tab:lux-profile} is the pessimistic bound}, which is
the useful direction for a figure that a criterion is checked against.

\section{Two definitions of contrast, and why there are two}
\label{sec:sim-contrast}

The contrast measurement used throughout this project discards samples whose
local background exceeds 20 levels, because in the orbital case and in the
studio with the tether at 5~m the only thing that can sit behind the cord and
be bright is a fixture in shot.

\textbf{With the tether at 1.5~m that threshold no longer means that.} The
floor enters the frame at 5.1~m, so behind the cord there is lit molton,
which passes 20 levels on its own. In the recommended arrangement the filter
discards 22\% of the tether and raises the contrast from 77.3 to 91.1; in the
side view it discards 100\% and returns no value at all.

Both are therefore reported. \textbf{Whole tether} is what the mask actually
has to solve. \textbf{Dark background} is what compares with everything
measured before the tether came down to 1.5~m. The ordering of the
arrangements is the same under both, so no conclusion in this chapter depends
on the choice.

\section{Looking at the renders rather than reading them}

Every variant in Table \ref{tab:sim-family} can be opened in the
application's \textbf{Simulation} page, tab \emph{Estudio TV2}, which lists
them with their stereo pair and their figures. It reads
\texttt{medidas\_familia.csv}, the same file this chapter's tables come
from, and recomputes nothing. The images it shows carry a gamma curve so
they can be seen on a screen, and the page says so where they are shown: no
figure in this chapter comes from them.
